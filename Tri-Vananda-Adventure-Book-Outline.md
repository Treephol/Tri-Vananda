# The Three Friends of Tri Vananda
### *Adventures in the Whispering Forest*

A storybook for Tri Vananda kids' programming.
Target reader: ages 7–10. On-property use across forest lab, Sunday picnics, family reading hour, and welcome amenities for young residents and guests.

---

## 1. The Book Concept

The book borrows directly from the name itself — **Tri** (three / brothers), **Vana** (forest), **Nanda** (ultimate happiness) — and turns it into a living story three children can step into.

Three young friends meet on the land, explore it through a season of gentle adventures, and discover that the real magic of the place isn't any one thing — it's the way nature, community, and wellbeing quietly belong to each other.

This is not a marketing book dressed up as a story. It's a proper adventure, written to make children want to come back to the land, read it with their grandparents, and retell the story over a bonfire dinner.

The structure follows the brand adage — **Build · Belong · Bloom** — which becomes the three acts of the book without ever being named on the page.

---

## 2. The Characters

**Nara** (age 8) — Our point-of-view character. New to Tri Vananda. Curious, a little shy, quick to notice things others miss. Comes from a city; the forest is new to her.

**Ploy** (age 7) — Nara's younger sister. Imaginative and brave in her own quiet way. Believes the forest talks to her.

**Arun** (age 9) — A boy from the nearby village. Knows the land, the trees, the lakes, the animals. Generous with what he knows. Always has a mango in his pocket.

**Grandpa Tong** — The wise elder the children keep bumping into. A gardener, a storyteller, a teacher of small things. Represents the quiet hand of generations before.

**Moo the Buffalo** — A gentle old water buffalo who lives by the lakes. Says nothing, notices everything.

**Kai the Hornbill** — A playful, mischievous hornbill who leaves riddles on leaves for the children to find. The book's guide and trickster.

**Tua-Tua the Tree Frog** — A tiny green frog who shows up at the most surprising moments. Comic relief. Loved by young readers.

## 3. The Full Book Structure

### PART ONE — BUILD (chapters 1–3)
*Meeting the land, meeting each other.*

**Chapter 1 — A New Home Under the Mango Trees**
Nara arrives at Tri Vananda with her family. First impressions: the air, the green, the enormous mango tree outside her window. Meets Ploy exploring the garden. They find Tua-Tua the frog on a leaf.

**Chapter 2 — The Boy From the Village**
A boy appears at the gate with a bicycle and a basket of mangoes. This is Arun. He offers them fruit, then disappears into the trees. Nara and Ploy decide to follow.

**Chapter 3 — The Morning Stretch Under the Tamarind**
The three children, now friends, meet Grandpa Tong under an ancient tamarind tree. He teaches them how to stretch like the trees do — roots down, branches up. A gentle first lesson in being still.

---

### PART TWO — BELONG (chapters 4–8)
*Adventures together, deepening bonds.*

**Chapter 4 — The Secret of the Nine Lakes**
The friends set out to map every one of the nine lakes on the land. They meet Moo the Buffalo at the third lake. At the fifth, Kai the Hornbill drops a leaf with strange writing on it — a riddle.

**Chapter 5 — The Forest Lab**
Chasing the first clue leads them into the Forest Lab — a place of leaves, roots, and jars filled with scents. Grandpa Tong teaches them the names of plants and what each one does. Some heal you. Some feed you. Some simply make the world beautiful.

**Chapter 6 — Kai's Map**
Kai drops a second leaf. Then a third. Each is a riddle that leads further into the forest. The children realise Kai is making them a map only they can read.

**Chapter 7 — The Great Bonfire Night**
Evening at the community fire. Families from all over the land gather. The children hear old stories told by elders. The adults dance. Ploy falls asleep against Grandpa Tong. Arun and Nara stay awake watching the stars.

**Chapter 8 — Cooking With Grandpa Tong**
Morning in the farm kitchen. The children pick what they'll eat. Grandpa Tong teaches them how to tear, not cut, Thai basil; how to pound garlic gently; how food tastes different when you grew it yourself. They feed the grown-ups.

---

### PART THREE — BLOOM (chapters 9–12)
*Growing into the place. Giving something back.*

**Chapter 9 — Zen Archery and the Still Mind**
The friends are taught archery — not to hit the target, but to find stillness before the arrow leaves. Nara's arrow misses. Her second one lands. She learns why.

**Chapter 10 — The Silent Walk**
The final riddle leads them to a silent walk through the forest at dawn. No talking for one hour. They hear things they've never heard before. The forest *is* whispering — you just have to stop making noise to hear it.

**Chapter 11 — The Festival of Lights**
The community celebrates the turning of the season. Lanterns on the lakes. Music under the stars. All three generations — children, parents, grandparents — together in one evening. The friends understand, for the first time, what *belonging* actually feels like.

**Chapter 12 — The Promise Tree**
On the last page of the season, the three friends plant a small tree together. They promise to come back each year to see how it grows. Kai watches from a branch. Tua-Tua hops onto the sapling's leaf. Grandpa Tong smiles. The story closes where it began — under a tree, with the three friends, in the quiet hum of the forest.

## 4. Supplementary Materials *(the last third of the book)*

These transform the storybook into a companion for actual on-property kids' programming.

**The Illustrated Map of Tri Vananda** — A friendly, hand-drawn map of the land the children can use to find the places from the book in real life. Spread across two pages.

**The Field Guide** — Illustrated pages naming the plants, birds, and animals the children meet in the story. Written so a child can identify them on a forest walk. Includes Thai names alongside English.

**A Few Words in Thai** — A little glossary of the Thai words used in the book (*sawasdee, kob khun, ma-muang* — mango, *ton-mai* — tree). Small and warm; invites the children into the language of the place.

**Your Turn** — Four activity pages for the children to personalise the book:
*Draw your own riddle leaf.*
*Name your favourite tree.*
*Write a letter to Tua-Tua.*
*Plan your own adventure.*

**A Note to the Grown-Up Reading This** — A single-page note for parents and grandparents. Gentle suggestions on how to read the book aloud, and questions to ask children between chapters — the kind of questions that open conversations about nature, family, and care.

## 5. Tone & Voice *(on-brand for Tri Vananda)*

**Reading age:** 7–10, but written so grandparents reading aloud won't feel they're reading something beneath them.

**Language:** Warm, sensory, unhurried. Never clinical, never cute-for-cute's-sake. Trust the child reader.

**Pacing:** Short chapters (~800–1,200 words each). Each chapter ends on a small turn — a discovery, a question, a quiet moment — that invites the next page.

**Nature is a character.** Not a backdrop, not a setting. Trees, animals, water, and weather have agency in the story. The land is someone the children are getting to know.

**Multigenerational by design.** Grandpa Tong is as important as any child character. The book reinforces that wisdom, play, and wonder move across generations — which is the brand's quietest and deepest claim.

**Wellness is lived, not taught.** No character says the word "wellness." Stretching, eating real food, breathing, being quiet, noticing — these are things the children *do*, not things they are lectured about. The reader absorbs the way of living the same way the children in the story do.

## 6. Production Notes

**Format:** Hardcover, approx. 24 × 24 cm square format (a comfortable size for a child to hold and for a grandparent to read aloud). Uncoated, warm paper stock — feels like it belongs on the land.

**Length:** Approximately 80 pages total — 12 chapters (roughly 4–6 pages each with illustrations) + 15 pages of supplementary materials + front/back matter.

**Illustration direction:** Hand-drawn cartoon style — soft, warm, painterly. Leans toward the work of Oliver Jeffers, Beatrice Alemagna, or the gentle naturalism of a Studio Ghibli opening sequence. Characters expressive but not over-stylised. Nature always given proper visual weight.

**Colour palette:** Anchored in the Tri Vananda brand — forest green, warm terracotta-red, sun-beige, soft off-white — with seasonal shifts (golden morning, rainy green, evening firelight, starry dusk).

**Typography:** Warm serif for body text (something like Fraunces or Adobe Caslon). Hand-lettered feel for chapter titles and Kai's leaf-riddles.

## 7. How the Book Fits On-Property Programming

The book isn't designed to live on a shelf. Each chapter corresponds to a real activity already happening at Tri Vananda:

| Chapter | Kids' Programming Touchpoint |
|---|---|
| Ch. 3 — Stretching under the tamarind | Morning family yoga |
| Ch. 4 — The Nine Lakes | Kayak & lake exploration |
| Ch. 5 — The Forest Lab | Forest Lab session |
| Ch. 7 — Bonfire Night | Community bonfire gathering |
| Ch. 8 — Cooking with Grandpa Tong | Farm-to-kitchen cooking class |
| Ch. 9 — Zen Archery | Zen archery session |
| Ch. 10 — Silent Walk | Forest bathing |
| Ch. 11 — Festival of Lights | Seasonal community gathering |
| Ch. 12 — Promise Tree | Tree-planting ritual for young guests and residents |

The book becomes the **narrative backbone** of the kids' programme. Children read the chapter, then live it.

## 8. What Comes After

Once Book One is established, natural extensions include:

— **Book Two — Moo's Story** (told from the old buffalo's perspective — a year at Tri Vananda across the seasons).
— **Book Three — Grandpa Tong's Garden** (the elder's backstory — recipes, remedies, quiet wisdom).
— A seasonal **Adventure Booklet** the children receive on each stay or as residents — a shorter, activity-based follow-on.
— A **family journal edition** that pairs the story with blank pages for children to record their own adventures on the land.
