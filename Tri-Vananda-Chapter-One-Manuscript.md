# The Three Friends of Tri Vananda
## Chapter One — A New Home Under the Mango Trees

*An interactive chapter with option paths. Middle-reader age 7–10.*
*Word count: ~3,100 words across all paths. Each reader experiences roughly ~1,800 words in one read.*

---

### How the option paths work

The chapter has **one opening** every reader sees, then **two choice moments** where the reader picks a path. The paths quietly reconnect — no matter which route the child takes, they arrive at the same ending, but with different small discoveries on the way.

This means:

- A child reading the book alone chooses their own way through it.
- Siblings or friends reading together can compare what they saw on different paths.
- The same chapter can be re-read three or four times before a child has found everything.

The structure below uses simple signposts so a grown-up reading aloud can navigate easily.

```
OPENING  →  CHOICE 1  →  Path A  ┐
                     →  Path B  ┴→  MIDDLE  →  CHOICE 2  →  Path C  ┐
                                                          →  Path D  ┴→  ENDING
```

---

## § OPENING — *Everyone reads this.*

Nara pressed her nose to the car window.

The road had been winding for a long time, and her little sister Ploy was asleep on the seat next to her — mouth a tiny bit open, cheek squashed against the seatbelt, one sandal fallen off.

"Are we there?" Nara asked.

Her father smiled without turning his head. "Look up."

Nara looked up.

And there, rising out of the road ahead, was a tree. Not just a tree. A *tree*. The biggest tree Nara had ever seen. Its trunk was as wide as her bedroom, and its branches reached out like the arms of a grandmother welcoming the family home. And in its branches — oh, in its branches — were mangoes. Dozens. Hundreds. Each one glowing like a little orange sun, as if the tree had borrowed the colour of the whole day's sunset and hidden it away in its leaves.

"Is that where we live?" Nara whispered.

"That's the gate," said her father. "Our house is just past it."

The car rolled slowly underneath the great tree. A mango — just one — fell softly onto the roof of the car with a *thump*. Ploy woke up.

"Did someone throw a rock?" she asked sleepily.

"No," said Nara. "The tree gave us a present."

The car stopped.

Nara opened the door and stepped out — and the first thing that happened was that she forgot to breathe. Because the air was different. It smelled *green*. It smelled wet. It smelled like a thousand things she did not yet know the names of.

She breathed in.

Somewhere, a bird made a sound like a little silver bell.

Somewhere else, far off, water moved.

"Welcome home," said her mother from the door of the house.

Nara didn't move. She didn't want to. She wanted to stand in the air a little longer.

And then, from inside, her mother called again.

*"Nara? Can you bring your bag?"*

Nara picked up her small red suitcase. But before she went inside, she looked around. The garden was waiting. The mango tree was waiting. And from behind a bush — or was she imagining it? — something small and green and quiet was waiting too.

She had a choice to make.

> Does Nara explore the garden first,
> or does she go inside to find her new room?

*Readers, choose your path.*

## § PATH A — *The Garden* (if Nara stays outside)

Nara set the suitcase down by the step.

"I'll come in a minute, Mum!" she called. "I just want to look!"

She stepped off the path and into the grass.

The grass was soft. Not the hard, tired grass of a city park — this grass was thick and full and smelled like afternoons. She walked slowly, because she didn't want to scare anything.

That is when she saw Ploy. Who had apparently woken up properly and escaped from the car without anyone noticing. Ploy was crouched in the middle of the garden, as still as a statue, staring at a leaf as if the leaf were trying to tell her an enormous secret.

"Ploy?" whispered Nara. "What are you doing?"

Ploy did not move. She only lifted one finger and pointed.

Nara bent down.

And there, in the middle of a wide green leaf, sat a frog. The smallest frog in the whole world. He was perfectly green, like a bead of dew that had learned how to sit very still. His eyes were round and bright, and he was looking at Ploy. Ploy was looking at him.

"I think," said Ploy in her most serious voice, "that he is trying to tell me something."

"What do you think he's saying?" whispered Nara.

Ploy thought about this for a long time.

"He's saying," she said finally, "that his name is Tua-Tua. And that he lives here. And that we are welcome."

Nara smiled. She had never before met a frog who had a name. But somehow, looking at this particular frog, it seemed exactly right.

Tua-Tua blinked once — slowly, carefully, with the dignity of someone who has lived in a garden for a long time — and then hopped off the leaf. He landed in the grass and, without making a single sound, disappeared into the ferns.

"Wait!" said Ploy. "Come back!"

"He will," said Nara, though she wasn't sure how she knew. "I think he just has things to do."

The sisters sat for a moment in the warm grass. A breeze moved through the mango tree and the leaves whispered to each other like a classroom of children who had been asked to be quiet but couldn't.

And then, from the direction the frog had hopped, came a new sound.

*Squeak — squeak — squeak.*

Like old wheels. Coming closer.

## § PATH B — *The Window* (if Nara goes inside)

Nara carried her small red suitcase through the doorway.

The house was big and full of light. There were wooden floors that had been polished to the colour of honey, and tall windows that looked out at the garden, and a ceiling fan that turned slowly in the warm afternoon air. It smelled like lemongrass and old wood and something else — something like rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"Your room is at the top of the stairs," her mother said. "I thought you'd like the one with the window that opens."

Nara climbed the stairs.

She found her room.

And she forgot about everything else.

Because her room had a window. And the window had a view. And the view was — the tree. The enormous mango tree, right there, so close that she could almost reach out and touch its leaves. It was as if the tree had grown in that exact spot, over a hundred years, just so it could one day say hello to whoever would live in this room.

Nara put the suitcase on the bed and opened the window.

The air that came in was thick with the smell of ripe fruit and damp earth. A butterfly the colour of old gold drifted past. A bird she had never seen before — with a bright red breast and a tiny black eye — landed on a branch, tilted its head at her, and then flew off again, as if it had only wanted to check.

And then, down in the garden, something moved.

It was small. It was green. It was hopping.

And beside it — or near it — was something bigger. A shadow that wasn't quite a bird and wasn't quite anything else. It was there, and then it wasn't, as if the garden had briefly opened a door and then shut it again.

Nara leaned out further.

"*I think he is trying to tell me something,*" said a voice — her sister Ploy's voice — from down below.

Ploy was crouched in the grass, talking in her most serious voice to something Nara couldn't quite see from the window.

Nara stepped back.

She would go down in a moment. But first — first she wanted to watch. Because from here, from her window, she could see the whole garden, and the whole garden seemed to be… doing something. Quietly. As if every single thing in it — the tree, the grass, the birds, even the light — was in on a very gentle secret.

And then, faintly, from the direction of the road, she heard it.

*Squeak — squeak — squeak.*

Like old wheels. Coming closer.

## § THE MIDDLE — *Both paths converge.*

Whether Nara had been kneeling in the garden or leaning out of her bedroom window, she was now looking toward the gate. Because something was coming.

It was a boy.

On a bicycle.

The bicycle was slightly too big for him, and it was painted a faded red, and its bell was broken. The boy had messy black hair and no shoes, and in the basket on the front of the bicycle was a wobbling pyramid of mangoes that was trying *very hard* not to fall out.

He stopped at the gate.

He looked at Nara.

Nara looked at him.

"You're new," he said.

"Yes," said Nara.

He reached into the basket and took out a mango. He held it out with both hands, like someone presenting something important.

"I'm Arun," he said. "This is for you."

Nara took the mango carefully. It was warm from the sun. It smelled the way she imagined happiness would smell, if happiness were a fruit.

"Thank you," she said.

"Do you like lakes?" Arun asked.

"I… I don't know," said Nara. "I've never really *had* a lake before."

Arun grinned. His grin had two missing teeth on the side.

"There are nine," he said. "I can show you. But not today. Today you have to unpack and sleep. Tomorrow — early, before the sun is really awake — meet me at the big tamarind."

"Where's the big tamarind?"

Arun looked at her as if she had asked where the sky was.

"*Everyone* knows where the big tamarind is."

And with that, he pushed off. *Squeak — squeak — squeak.* And his bicycle rolled away down the path, into the trees.

Nara stood there holding the mango.

She had another choice to make.

## § PATH C — *Follow Him* (if Nara runs after Arun)

"*Wait!*" Nara called. "Wait — I don't know where—"

But Arun was already gone.

Well — not quite gone. She could still hear the squeak-squeak-squeak of his bicycle, growing fainter through the trees.

Nara pushed the mango deep into her pocket. It was too big; it stuck out at an angle, like a small orange elbow. She began to run. Her sandals slapped on the path. Ploy shouted something behind her, but Nara did not stop.

The path curved between tall bushes, and then between even taller trees, and then — quite suddenly — it opened into a wide clearing. And in the middle of the clearing was a tree.

*The* tree.

It was not as big as the mango tree at the gate. It was bigger. Its trunk was so thick that three children standing in a circle would not have been able to join hands around it. Its branches spread wider than a house. Underneath it, the light was cool and green, and moved like water moved.

Arun was there. He had stopped his bicycle and was standing very quietly, looking up into the branches.

"Is this it?" Nara whispered. "Is this the tamarind?"

Arun nodded, without looking at her.

"Why are we whispering?" whispered Nara.

"Because," said Arun, "this tree is older than both our grandmothers. You don't shout near old things."

Nara held her breath.

There was no wind in the clearing. None at all. And yet somehow, the leaves of the tamarind were moving. Just a little. As if the tree itself were breathing. As if it were listening to them both.

And then — high up among the leaves — something let go.

A single leaf.

It spun down slowly. Down and down and down. And it landed, quite lightly, on the top of Nara's head.

Arun grinned.

"That means the tree likes you," he said.

"How do you know?"

"Because it's true."

Nara took the leaf off her head and held it in her palm. It was shaped like a teardrop. And on its surface — she looked closer — were tiny marks. Thin lines. Almost like writing. But surely that couldn't be. Surely leaves didn't—

"Come on," said Arun suddenly, jumping back onto his bicycle. "Your mum will be looking for you. I'll be here tomorrow."

He rode off without saying goodbye, which Nara didn't mind, because she was still looking at the leaf.

She put it carefully in her pocket, next to the mango.

As she turned to walk back, she thought she saw — just for a second — a flash of black and white and yellow, high up in the tamarind. A bird with a very large, very curved beak. Watching her.

But when she looked again, it was gone.

## § PATH D — *Stay With the Tree* (if Nara lets Arun go)

Nara didn't chase him.

She didn't know why. It would have been easy. But somehow, the idea of staying felt more important than the idea of following. So instead, she walked back to the enormous mango tree at the gate, and she sat down against its trunk.

The bark was warm against her back. The ground was cool under her knees. She put the mango in her lap and looked up at the tree — which, from here, was a great green cathedral, every leaf whispering to every other leaf in a language she did not yet know.

She closed her eyes.

The mango tree breathed.

At least — that was what it felt like. A slow *in* and a slow *out*, that was not her breathing, because her breathing was quick and small. This was bigger. This was the breathing of something that had been alive for a very, very long time.

Somewhere, far off, a bird sang three notes.

Somewhere nearer, her sister Ploy was still talking, in her serious voice, to something that might have been a frog.

And Nara felt — for the first time since the long car ride, since the packing of boxes, since the saying goodbye to her old house, since everything — that she was in *exactly the right place*. Not the right *house*, exactly. The right *place*. As if some small, lost part of her had been waiting here all along, and had just now been found.

She opened her eyes.

A leaf was drifting down.

It spiralled gently through the warm air. It landed on her lap — right next to the mango. As if it had been aiming.

Nara picked it up.

It was shaped like a teardrop. And on its surface, if she looked carefully — if she held it up to the fading afternoon light — were tiny marks. Thin, neat lines. Almost like writing. Almost like—

"*Nara?*" called her mother from the house. "Come inside please, it's getting dark!"

Nara folded the leaf carefully and put it in her pocket, next to the mango.

As she stood up, she thought she heard — just faintly, above her — the sound of wings. A slow, heavy, unhurried sound. Something big was up in the mango tree.

She tilted her head back.

The leaves moved. But nothing else did.

And yet, as she walked toward the house, Nara was quite certain — although she could not have said how — that somebody, or some*thing*, had just watched her go.

## § THE ENDING — *All paths meet here.*

That night — after the unpacking and the dinner, after the stars had come out (and there were *so many stars*, more stars than Nara had ever seen, so many that the sky seemed to have been sprinkled with salt) — the two sisters sat on the edge of Nara's new bed.

Ploy had something in her hand.

"Look," she whispered. "Tua-Tua dropped this. I think. Or it fell. I don't know. But I found it."

She held out a leaf.

It was shaped like a teardrop.

And on its surface, in tiny, careful handwriting, were four words:

> *Tomorrow.*
> *The nine lakes.*

Nara's hand went slowly to her own pocket.

She drew out the leaf *she* had found — the one from the tamarind, or the one from the mango tree, depending on which way her afternoon had gone — and laid it on the bed beside Ploy's.

They were the same leaf.

The same shape. The same handwriting.

The two sisters stared.

"Who wrote it?" whispered Ploy.

"I don't know."

"Do we go?"

Nara thought about the air that smelled green, and the boy on the squeaking bicycle, and the tree older than grandmothers. She thought about the shadow at the window, and the frog who had a name. She thought about how the mango tree had given them a present the moment they arrived.

"We go," she said.

Somewhere outside the window, an owl called softly. And somewhere further away, something with wings — something big — flew from one tree to another, making a sound like wings clapping.

Ploy got into bed. Nara turned off the light.

But before she went to sleep, she laid both leaves side by side on the bedside table — the one Ploy had found, and the one that had found her.

"Goodnight, mango tree," she whispered.

The mango tree said nothing.

But outside the window, a warm wind moved through its leaves — and somehow, Nara was absolutely sure, it was saying goodnight back.

*— End of Chapter One —*

## § A NOTE FOR THE GROWN-UP READING THIS

*If you are reading the book aloud with a child, here are some gentle questions to ask between paths:*

After **Choice One** — *"Would you have gone into the garden, or up to the window?"*

After **Choice Two** — *"Would you have run after Arun, or stayed by the tree?"*

After **The Ending** — *"What do you think the message on the leaf means? Who do you think wrote it?"*

These are not tests. They are invitations. The magic of this book is that every child reads a slightly different version of it.
